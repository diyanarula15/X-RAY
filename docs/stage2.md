# Stage 2 — real data and the interactive instrument

Stage 1 proved the method in a simulator we controlled. Stage 2 runs it on real
2026 Formula 1 telemetry and replaces the scripted video with something a
skeptical judge can drive.

Everything below is measured. `docs/feasibility.md` is generated by
`scripts/feasibility.py`; the race numbers come from `scripts/analyse_race.py`.

---

## What the feasibility study changed

The spec put a gate before the UI: run four reality checks first and report.
Two came back badly, and both changed the build.

**The calibration window does not exist on real circuits.** Stage 1 identified
drag above 340 km/h, where the regulatory taper has squeezed deployment to
almost nothing. Across six 2026 races, between **0.00% and 0.11%** of samples
clear 340 km/h — a few hundred out of 400,000, spread over twenty cars. Monaco
tops out at 292 km/h with nothing above 290. A binary speed gate does not
survive contact with real telemetry anywhere, so §2's generalized calibration
stopped being optional.

**The sample rate sits on the cliff edge.** Real 2026 telemetry arrives
irregularly at a median of **4.17 Hz** at every circuit probed, with a p90 gap of
0.36–0.40 s and holes over a second about once a lap. Stage 1's own ablation put
the accuracy cliff at about 4 Hz. There is no margin, and the app says so on its
front page rather than in a footnote.

Two checks came back well. FastF1's position stream carries a live **Z channel**
(Spa reports 102 m of elevation, which is correct), so gradient is real and so is
the 3D track. And **DRS is identically zero in 2026**, as expected — while
throttle, brake, gear and RPM *are* populated. That is more than Stage 1 assumed,
and it is worth being precise about what it changes: regime classification can
now be read off the feed instead of inferred. **There is still no energy,
deployment or state-of-charge channel. That absence is the entire premise.**

## The generalized calibration

The replacement for the speed gate is an interval formulation. For a given wind
offset, observed wheel power is *linear* in drag area, and the regulation bounds
total wheel power from both sides:

```
   -P_MGUK_MAX  <=  A(k) + CdA*B(k)  <=  (P_ICE_MAX + ceiling(v_k))*eta
```

Rearranged, **every sample brackets CdA**:

```
   (-P_MGUK_MAX - A)/B   <=   CdA   <=   ((P_ICE_MAX + ceiling)*eta - A)/B
```

There is no threshold anywhere in that. A sample at 340 km/h has a near-zero
ceiling and pins CdA between two almost identical numbers; a sample at 120 km/h
has a 350 kW ceiling and barely constrains it at all. The estimator uses all of
them, each weighted by how binding it actually is, and **the width of the
surviving interval is the identifiability score.**

Both bounds come from the rulebook rather than from a model of the engine. That
distinction was not free: an earlier version inferred ICE output from the
throttle trace and produced lower bounds of CdA > 13, because a real car at
30 m/s is traction-limited rather than power-limited and no throttle-to-power
curve survives that.

The bounds are then sharpened where the feed removes ambiguity. Brakes on drops
the lower bound, because brake force is not in the model. Throttle off drops the
400 kW term, which tightens the upper bound severalfold — **the coast-down
channel, and unlike a high-speed window it exists at every circuit on earth.**

Finally, twenty cars run the same regulations through the same air, so the
identified sets are pooled across the field. A car with no clean high-speed
running inherits the field constraint and is flagged as having done so, in the
UI, on its own panel.

### Measured

| circuit | identifiability | pooled CdA | cars | refused |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Zandvoort | 89% | 0.92 m² | 20 | 17 |
| Melbourne | 81% | 1.14 m² | 20 | 15 |
| Spa-Francorchamps | 77% | 0.84 m² | 21 | 16 |
| Silverstone | **0%** | — | 22 | 20 |
| Monte Carlo | **0%** | — | 20 | 20 |

Spa's pooled drag area of 0.84 m² is a real F1 figure, and its per-lap energy
comes out at **3.7 MJ deployed against 3.5 MJ recovered** — energy-neutral,
inside a 4 MJ store, which is what real energy management looks like.

Monaco and Silverstone return nothing, and that is the result, not a failure. A
circuit that never reaches high speed cannot pin drag area from a speed trace.
The method declines instead of guessing, with no per-circuit tuning anywhere.

Air density is computed from each session's own weather rather than assumed:
1.159 kg/m³ at Spa against the 1.20 Stage 1 used. That 3.5% would otherwise have
landed directly on CdA.

## The real-data validation, and why it is a null

There is no public ground-truth energy channel for a real car, so the estimates
cannot be checked directly. What can be checked is a prediction the *regulation*
makes: a car within 1.000 s of the car ahead at the detection point becomes
eligible for extra deployment, and a car at 1.001 s does not.

Over five races and **1,851 car-laps**, the discontinuity at that boundary is
**−0.20 MJ, SE 0.242, p = 0.41**. A null.

The design itself is sound. The McCrary density shows no manipulation of the
running variable, and both race position and lap number are balanced across the
boundary. But the **minimum detectable effect is 0.68 MJ against a Manual
Override allocation of 0.5 MJ**. The study is underpowered, so the null says
something about how many races we have analysed and nothing about whether the
regulation does anything. Roughly twice the data would resolve it.

The app reports the power calculation next to the estimate every time, because a
null without one is close to meaningless. The significance-versus-cutoff strip
shows the other half: the scan is flat, and there is a spurious p = 0.04 at
1.30 s — thirteen tests, one false positive, exactly as expected. That is the
strip earning its place.

Two bugs found on the way, both of which produced plausible-looking nonsense
before they were caught: gap indices computed before a sort and used after it,
and a running variable that was the difference of two cars' *lap times* rather
than the gap between them — a quantity that is equal for two cars a pit stop
apart, and which made the boundary unfindable by construction.

## The interface

React, TypeScript, Vite. React Three Fiber for the scene, D3 for every
statistical view, zustand for one shared clock, Framer Motion for overlays.

**One clock.** A single store holds race time and playback state; the 3D scene,
every overlay and every chart subscribe to it. Nothing keeps its own timer.

**Spectacle only where the spectacle is the data.** The 3D view is immersive
because deployment painted onto real circuit geometry, and a particle filter
rendered as particles, *are* the findings. The RDD explorer, the ablation curve,
the fingerprint radar and Method & Limits are flat 2D with no effects at all —
their authority comes from looking as though they were not art-directed.

### The six views

1. **Race theatre** — scrubbable replay on the real circuit, belief cloud beside
   the rival.
2. **Observability map** — the circuit coloured by what the estimator can learn
   where. La Source and the Bus Stop chicane come out red; the Kemmel straight
   comes out green. Partial observability as a picture of a racetrack.
3. **RDD explorer** — the falsification instrument. Drag the cutoff and hunt for
   an effect where the rules do not put one.
4. **Decision explorer** — the threshold, the opportunity, and the fan of 200
   re-solved opponent policies behind it.
5. **Opponent fingerprint** — deployment style per driver, tightening as evidence
   accumulates. On Spa the two drivers separate: 75% aggression against 45%, and
   a visibly larger held buffer on the second.
6. **Method & limits** — what the system cannot do, stated first.

### The belief cloud

The signature visual is not a bar with a shaded region. It is the particle
filter: 400 points on a vertical energy axis beside the rival's car, each at its
own estimate of deployable energy, contracting where the trace is informative and
dispersing where it is not.

One bug in it is worth recording, because it is the kind that looks like nothing:
the spring interpolation used `cur[i] || target` to detect an uninitialised
particle, which treats a legitimate 0.0 as unset. Every particle stayed pinned at
the world origin, and the cloud rendered as a small orange smudge in the middle
of the circuit that was easy to mistake for an intentional marker.

### Performance and the room

`?lite=1` drops postprocessing, cuts the cloud to 120 particles and flattens the
trails. Fonts are a system stack, not a webfont, so nothing is fetched at load;
all assets are bundled and the app runs fully offline. The car is extruded
geometry built in code — no liveried model, no licensing question.

The track ribbon is drawn 20 m wide rather than a true 12–15 m. It is the primary
data surface and the paint modes have to be readable from the tactical camera,
where a true width is sub-pixel. It is the only schematic liberty in the scene.

## Running it

```bash
pip install -r requirements.txt          # adds fastf1, fastapi, uvicorn
python scripts/feasibility.py --round 10 # the four reality checks
python scripts/feasibility_report.py     # -> docs/feasibility.md
python scripts/analyse_race.py --round 10
python -m uvicorn api.main:app --port 8011

cd app && npm install && npm run build   # the API then serves it at /
```

Open <http://127.0.0.1:8011/>. Add `?lite=1` for the borrowed-laptop case.

## What is still missing

- Five races is not enough to power the RDD. Roughly ten would be.
- Aero-mode zones are hand-entered per circuit; the FIA publishes them as PDFs.
- The gap used as the RDD running variable is measured at the start/finish line,
  not at each Manual Override detection point. That adds noise and costs power.
- Car mass is a constant 790 kg. Fuel burn over a stint is real and is not
  modelled, which biases early-lap estimates.
