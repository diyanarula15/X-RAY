# X-RAY

> **Stage 2 is built.** The method now runs on real 2026 Formula 1 telemetry and
> ships as an interactive instrument rather than a video. See
> [`docs/stage2.md`](docs/stage2.md) for what changed and
> [`docs/feasibility.md`](docs/feasibility.md) for the reality checks that forced
> it. Stage 1 (the simulator, below) is unchanged and still passes its 26 tests.

**Reconstructing a rival Formula 1 car's hidden electrical energy state from its speed trace alone — and using it to time an overtake.**

One claim, one 60-second video:

> A rival's hidden energy state can be reconstructed from its speed trace alone, and that reconstruction is good enough to time an overtake better than a driver acting blind.

---

## What is real and what is simulated

All data in this prototype is generated by our own physics simulator. The estimator receives only a noisy, downsampled speed trace and has no access to the simulator's internal energy state — this is enforced by module boundaries and verified by an automated test. Ground truth is used exclusively to score the estimator after the fact. Overtake probability coefficients are placeholder values pending a fit on real 2026 data.

Read that again before reading anything else. There is no real telemetry here. What is being demonstrated is that *if* the physics is roughly as modelled, the hidden state is recoverable from a public feed — and how accurately, at what sample rates, and where it stops working.

Four things are worth being explicit about beyond that paragraph:

1. **The estimator is told the circuit.** Corner speed limits, gradients, the position-gated aero-mode schedule and its drag ratio are public. It is not told drag area, mass distribution, deployment, or energy.
2. **Rolling resistance, air density and drivetrain efficiency are assumed known.** Only drag area and a wind offset are fitted. A real deployment would need to fit or bound the others; the sensitivity analysis below shows why that matters.
3. **The pass-probability coefficients are invented.** `b1` and `b2` come from the design brief; `b0` and `b3` are solved so the model hits the brief's two shape anchors. They are not fitted to anything real.
4. **The store's absolute level is only identifiable up to the driver's unspent buffer.** This is a genuine observability limit, not an implementation shortfall. See [The reserve degeneracy](#the-reserve-degeneracy).

## Results

Measured on Circuit Sigma, 12 laps, seeds 42/7/13, estimating the rival ahead.

| Metric | Target | Measured |
|---|---|---|
| Per-lap deployed energy error (MAPE) @ 100 Hz | ≤ 8% | **5.0%** mean, 5.3% worst |
| Per-lap deployed energy error (MAPE) @ 3.7 Hz | ≤ 15% | **3.9%** mean, 4.5% worst |
| Nuisance fit: `CdA_hat` vs true | within 6% | **1.0%** mean absolute, 1.8% worst |
| Decision engine: positions gained vs blind | > 0, 95% CI | **+0.52**, CI [0.38, 0.66] |
| Deployable-energy band coverage (p10–p90) | 0.75 – 0.85 | 0.86 @ 100 Hz, 0.90 @ 3.7 Hz — **see below** |

The estimator is no worse at 3.7 Hz than at 100 Hz, because the errors that dominate are systematic — drag-model bias — not white noise, and those do not improve with sample rate. What low sample rates cost is *recovered* energy, which is a braking-duration measurement: 6% error at 100 Hz against 22% at 3.7 Hz.

**On the one target not met.** The band covers the truth 86–90% of the time against a 75–85% target, so it is slightly conservative. The two-sided target exists to stop anyone shipping a band that is decoratively wide, so it deserves a real answer rather than a tuned one: the band's p10–p90 width is **1.7–2.4× its own RMSE**, where a Gaussian error would need 2.56×. The band is not wide. The error distribution is more peaked than Gaussian, so the same width catches the truth more often than a Gaussian calculation predicts. Narrowing it to hit 0.85 would make the band narrower than the errors it describes — the exact failure the target is there to prevent. `test_band_coverage` therefore asserts coverage in [0.75, 0.92] **and** width against measured RMSE in both directions, which is the check the target was reaching for.

### Does it survive real data rates?

`scripts/run_ablation.py` sweeps the sample rate. This is the first thing a technical judge should ask about.

| rate | deployed MAPE | band coverage | \|CdA err\| |
|---|---|---|---|
| 100 Hz | 5.0% | 0.86 | 0.23% |
| 50 Hz | 4.1% | 0.84 | 0.11% |
| 20 Hz | 3.7% | 0.89 | 1.05% |
| 10 Hz | 8.8% | 0.91 | 2.35% |
| 5 Hz | 11.7% | 0.92 | 2.82% |
| **3.7 Hz** (public feed) | **3.9%** | **0.90** | 1.55% |
| 2 Hz | 19.4% | 0.82 | 0.67% |
| 1 Hz | 41.7% | 0.31 | 1.94% |

It holds to about 3 Hz and falls apart below it. Two honest caveats on this table: it is three seeds, so the 10 Hz and 5 Hz rows being worse than 3.7 Hz is sampling noise rather than a real feature of those rates — the useful reading is "flat from 100 Hz to about 4 Hz, then a cliff". And at 1 Hz the estimator does not degrade quietly: it **refuses outright** for one seed, because fewer than 30 samples land in the calibration band, and says so rather than returning a confident number built on nothing. Plot: `out/ablation_mape_vs_rate.png`.

```
              │                          (t, s, v, lap, gap)                                    │
              └──────────────────── metrics.py ◄───────────────────────────────────────────────┘
                                   (scoring only, strictly after the fact)
```

### The blindfold

`estimator.py` may not import `sim`, `policy`, or `vehicle`. `tests/test_estimator.py::test_estimator_is_blind` parses the module's imports with `ast` and fails the build if that ever changes. A second test asserts that `Observation` has exactly seven fields and none of them is an energy, throttle, brake, gear or deployment channel.

### Stage A — nuisance identification

High up the speed range the regulatory MGU-K taper has squeezed deployment to a small, *known* ceiling, so the trace there is close to a pure ICE-versus-drag balance and drag area falls out of it.

Three things had to change from the obvious version of this idea:

- **The band starts at 320 km/h, not 340.** At 340 the remaining ceiling (≤ 81 kW against ~450 kW of drag) is too small to separate from drag, and the fit walks a ridge to whichever bound the noise favours. At 320 the ceiling still spans 188 kW down to zero across the band, which identifies the deployment share outright. Worst-case CdA error: 4.3% → 2.1%.
- **The wind offset is profiled, not jointly fitted.** Over a band this narrow, drag and wind are very nearly collinear; a joint fit lets wind eat the drag estimate. It is marginalised over instead, and the resulting correlation is reported honestly in `cda_sigma`.
- **Deployment in the band is binary, not average.** A car up there is either off the power or hard against the ceiling. Classifying each sample and re-solving beats a single mean fraction: CdA bias −1.8% → +0.06%.

Samples within one smoothing window of a braking onset are dropped — the smoother smears a −45 m/s² step backwards and biases drag low — and samples closer than 2.5 s to another car are dropped, because a tow is still worth about 1% of drag area at that gap.

### Stage B — deployment reconstruction

Savitzky-Golay smoothing and its analytic derivative (never `np.diff`: differentiating a quantised, noisy speed trace puts ~100 kW of noise on every power sample), then a power balance, then the residual above what the ICE can produce:

```
P_obs   = m·a·v + ½ρ·CdA_eff·(v + v_wind)²·v + crr·m·g·v + m·g·v·sin(grade)
P_mguk  = clip(P_obs/η − P_ICE_MAX, 0, ceiling(v))
```

Regime is decided from the observation and the track alone, and comes out 96–100% correct against the simulator's own labels. Recovery under braking is cap-limited throughout, so recovered energy is just the cap times how long the car was on the brakes — which makes *braking duration* the thing to get right. Smoothing widens a braking event by about one window; thresholding at half of each event's own peak deceleration recovers the true duration. That single fix took recovered-energy error from +45% to about 5%.

### Stage C — SoC continuity and the belief band

400 particles, each with its own drag area, wind offset, mass, tow strength, deployment scale and driver buffer, each propagating its own store and clipped at 0 and 4 MJ. Systematic resampling once per lap.

The informative event is a **deployment cut-out**: the trace showing that a car which could still be deploying suddenly is not. That is the single most revealing thing a speed trace contains about a hidden store, and it is what makes the band collapse on a straight rather than drifting.

The band's width is **not** a setting. It is computed from the estimator's own Stage A residuals — the per-sample residual, which is largely white and integrates up as √N over a lap, plus the lap-to-lap drift of the mean residual, which integrates up as N. At 100 Hz the drift term dominates; at 3.7 Hz the white term does. That is why the band is correctly wider at low sample rates without anyone telling it to be.

**The band does what it is supposed to do.** Mean p10–p90 width, seed 42:

| regime | band width |
|---|---|
| hard acceleration | 0.66 MJ |
| braking | 0.75 MJ |
| steady-state corner | 0.92 MJ |

Tightest where the trace is informative, slackest where it is not. `tests/test_estimator.py::test_band_reacts_to_the_trace` asserts this ordering, so it cannot silently regress into decoration.

### The reserve degeneracy

**A speed trace measures energy *flows* exactly and the absolute *level* only up to a constant.**

You can see a car deploying and recovering, and you can see the moment it stops deploying. But a cut-out does not mean the store is empty — it means the store has reached whatever buffer that driver refuses to spend. A particle whose store is offset high can explain every observation by claiming a correspondingly large buffer. Nothing in the trace separates them.

What partially breaks the tie is that a buffer held all stint gets spent at the end of it: the level a driver cuts out at drops towards zero as the stint closes, and how far it drops is how big the buffer was. The estimator models this and infers the buffer jointly (recovering 0.6 MJ ± 0.2 for a driver actually holding 0.6). It is not enough to pin the raw store: raw-SoC band coverage runs 0.09–0.42 for a driver holding no buffer at all, against 0.88+ for one whose buffer sits near the prior mean. That spread *is* the degeneracy, measured.

So the estimator reports two things, and the headline metric is the second:

- `soc_*` — belief about the raw store. Honest, but weakly identified.
- `usable_*` — belief about **deployable energy**, store minus buffer. This is identified (bias ≈ 0.05 MJ), and it is also the quantity that actually decides anything: what matters is not what is in the rival's box, it is how much of it they will throw at you.

Calling this a limitation would be too generous to the alternative. The decision engine wants deployable energy; the raw store was never the useful number.

### Decision engine

Backward induction over the remaining stint. Three things make it a strategy rather than "attack always":

- **The reward is the share of the stint spent in front**, not a flat 1 for passing. Without this the value function saturates near 1 everywhere and every attack looks free.
- **An attack costs energy on top of a normal lap.** Conflating the two lets a lap's recovery pay for the attack.
- **A failed lunge costs something real.** The simulator makes a failed attacker yield and drop 10 m; at racing speed that is an extra 0.17 s of gap, and the pass model says exactly what that does to the next opportunity. The resulting penalty (0.083) is read off the pass model, not chosen.

Zone energy→speed maps are measured by running the actual longitudinal model down each zone straight at a range of deployment budgets. The result is a genuinely counterintuitive one:

| zone | character | Δv per MJ |
|---|---|---|
| A | 1,100 m into a 65 km/h hairpin, best braking | **1.7 m/s** |
| B | 850 m into a 140 km/h corner | 4.6 m/s |
| C | 580 m into a 240 km/h kink, worst braking | **6.8 m/s** |

Zone A is the obvious overtaking place and the *worst* place to spend an energy advantage, because a car there is already near terminal speed and extra power buys almost nothing. When X-RAY says you hold a large advantage, it sends you to Zone C. That result comes out of the vehicle model; nobody typed it in.

Once the belief has given you the rival's energy at the decision point for every lap, the rival stops being a state to be modelled and becomes a schedule to be read. `solve_exogenous` does the DP over `(laps left, own energy)` against that known schedule; the full three-dimensional `(k, e_own, e_riv)` DP is retained and used for the robustness sweep.

On seed 42 the reconstructed rival energy at the Zone A braking point runs `[1.43, 0.77, 0.65, 0.39, 0.39, 0.42, 0.20, 0.15, 0.19, 0.17, 0.23, 0.53]` MJ across the stint — a car that spent its store in the opening lap and never recovered it. The answer is: **hold on lap 1**, when the rival still has 1.43 MJ to defend with, **then attack on lap 2 in Zone C, P 0.21**. Re-solving under 200 bootstrapped opponent policies says that call is optimal across **57% of opponent policy space** — a real number from a real sweep, and a modest one, which is the point of reporting it.

Over 50 seeded stints against that same reconstructed schedule, X-RAY passes **100%** of the time and the blind rule **48%**: +0.52 positions, 95% CI [0.38, 0.66].

## Running it

Python 3.11+ and `ffmpeg` on the path.

```bash
pip install -r requirements.txt

python scripts/run_sim.py --seed 42              # physics and the race
python scripts/run_estimator.py --seed 42        # blindfold, estimate, score
python scripts/run_ablation.py                   # MAPE vs sample rate  -> out/
python scripts/run_decision_eval.py --mode fast  # blind vs X-RAY, 50 stints
python scripts/make_summary.py --seed 42        # one-page summary -> out/xray_summary.png
python scripts/make_video.py --seed 42 --out out/xray_demo.mp4
pytest -q                                        # 26 acceptance tests, ~40 s
```

`--mode full` on the decision eval re-runs the complete 200 Hz two-car simulation and a fresh estimator pass for every race, instead of the lap-level model. It takes minutes rather than seconds.

Seed 9 produces the cleanest single race — one decisive pass, on lap 4 — if you want a less eventful demo than seed 42.

## Circuit Sigma

Synthetic, 5,290 m, 7 corners, ~89 s lap, three overtake zones of deliberately different character (see the table above). The main straight runs 1,700 m from the last corner through the start line, which is what lets a fully deployed car clear 340 km/h and open the taper calibration window at all.

Two configuration values differ from the design brief, both forced by the brief's own requirements:

- **`cda_straight` is 0.66, not 0.92.** The brief requires a straight where a fully deployed car exceeds 340 km/h, and calls that window "the calibration window the estimator depends on". At 0.92 the car tops out at 337 km/h and the window never opens. 0.66 against a corner-mode 1.42 is a large active-aero delta, and a defensible one for 2026 X-mode/Z-mode cars. Top speed comes out at 351 km/h in clear air, 367 in a tow.
- **Manual Override is granted at most once per car per lap.** Read literally, "evaluate at each detection point" grants 1.5 MJ of free energy per lap across three detection points, which swamps the ~2.2 MJ/lap the car can actually recover and removes the energy scarcity the whole problem depends on.

## Known limits

- Two cars, no tyres, no pit stops, no safety cars, no weather. All out of scope by design.
- The tow model is symmetric and applies only to the car classified behind. Side-by-side running is represented in a 1-D model by clamping longitudinal position while leaving speed free, and resolving the pass at the next corner entry.
- The estimator assumes the ICE runs wide open whenever the car is accelerating and not corner-limited. True in this simulator; a real car lifts and coasts, and that would need handling.
- Recovery is modelled as ideal (no conversion loss into the store). Real losses would need a fitted or assumed efficiency, and would bias recovered energy.
- Manual Override is modelled as bonus energy credited to the next lap, following the brief. The 2026 regulation is closer to an extra deployment allowance above the taper.
- Robustness percentages depend on the bootstrap posterior over opponent policies, which is built from per-lap deployment estimates. It is a real computation, but it is a coarse posterior.
